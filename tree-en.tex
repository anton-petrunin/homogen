\begin{abstract}
A metric space is said to be all-set-homogeneous if any isometry between its subsets can be extended to an isometry of the whole space.
We classify all-set-homogeneous geodesic spaces with locally unique geodesics.
\end{abstract}


A metric space is said to be \emph{all-set-homogeneous}
if any isometry between its subsets can be extended to an isometry of the whole space.

Examples of all-set-homogeneous spaces include complete simply-connected Riemannian manifolds with constant curvature and the circles with length metrics.
These will be referred further as \emph{classical spaces}.

Garrett Birkhoff proved that \emph{any all-set-homogeneous geodesic space with locally unique nonbifurcating geodesics is classical} \cite{birkhoff}.
Here we use the following terminology:
A \emph{geodesic} in a metric space $M$ is a distance-preserving map from a real interval to $M$.
The image of a geodesic with endpoints $x$ and $y$ can be denoted by $[xy]_M$ and it might be also called \emph{geodesic}.
If for any $x,y\in M$ there is a geodesic $[xy]_M$, then $M$ is called a \emph{geodesic space}.
If any point $p\in M$ admits a neighborhood $U$ such that the geodesic $[xy]_M$ is uniquely defined for any $x,y\in U$, then $M$ has \emph{locally unique geodesics}.
If $[px]_M\subset [py]_M$ or $[px]_M\supset [py]_M$ whenever $[px]_M\cap [py]_M$ contains a nontrivial geodesic $[pw]_M$, then we say that geodesics in $M$ are \emph{nonbifurcating}.

Recall that a uniquely geodesic space $T$ is a \emph{metric tree} if every geodesic triangle $[xyz]_T$ in $T$ is a \emph{tripod};
that is, the union of any two geodesics $[xy]_T$, and $[yz]_T$ contains $[xz]_T$.
The \emph{valence} of $x\in T$ is defined as the cardinality of connected components in $T\setminus \{x\}$.

For any cardinality $n\ge2$, there is a unique, up to isometry,
complete metric tree $\TT_n$ with valence $n$ at every point \cite{dyubina-polterovich}.
Moreover, $\TT_n$ is homogeneous, and if $n<\infty$, then it is all-set-homogeneous; the last statement was proved in \cite{lebedeva-petrunin2024}.

We conjecture that \emph{any geodesic all-set-homogeneous space is complete} and
\emph{any (complete) geodesic all-set-homogeneous space is a universal metric tree of finite valence or a classical space}.

\begin{thm}{Theorem}
Any geodesic all-set-homogeneous space with locally unique geodesics is a universal metric tree of finite valence or a classical space.
\end{thm}

A metric space is called \emph{$n$-point homogeneous}
if any isometry between its subsets with at most $n$ points in each can be extended to an isometry of the whole space.

\begin{thm}{Proposition}
Suppose $M$ is a 3-point homogeneous geodesic space with locally unique geodesics.
If it has bifurcating geodesics, then $M$ is locally a metric tree;
that is, any point of $M$ has a neighborhood isometric to a metric tree.
%Moreover, we can assume that valence of a point in the tree is at least~$3$.
\end{thm}

\parit{Proof.}
Homogeneity and local uniqueness of geodesics imply that there is $R>0$ such that if $|x-y|_M<R$, then $[xy]_M$ is uniquely defined.

Let $[px]_M$ and $[py]_M$ be bifurcating geodesics;
that is, $[px]_M\not\subset[py]_M$ and $[px]_M\not\supset[py]_M$, but there is a nontrivial geodesic $[pw]_M\subset [px]_M\cap[py]_M$.
We can assume that the geodesic segment $[pw]_M$ is maximal.
Let $\sigma_0$, $\sigma_1$, and $\sigma_2$ be the arc-length parametrizations of $[wp]_M$, $[wx]_M$, and $[wy]_M$ starting from~$w$; so, $w=\sigma_0(0)=\sigma_1(0)=\sigma_2(0)$.
Let us restrict each $\sigma_i$ to a segment $[0,\ell]$ for some $0<\ell<\tfrac R2$.
By uniqueness, we have that $\sigma_1(t)=\sigma_2(t)$ only if $t=0$.
In particular,
\[s(t)\df|\sigma_1(t)-\sigma_2(t)|_M>0\]
if $t>0$; here $|\ -\ |_M$ denotes the distance between points in $M$.

Clearly $s(0)=0$, and by the triangle inequality, $s\:[0,\ell]\to\RR$ is a $2$-Lipschitz function.
(For metric trees we must have $s(t)\equiv2\cdot t$.)

Observe that $s$ is strictly increasing.
Indeed, suppose $s(r_0)\ge s(r_1)$ for some $r_0<r_1$.
By the intermediate value theorem we can find $r_0<r_2\le r_1$ such that $s(r_0)=s(r_2)$.
Then
\[\sigma_1(r_0)\mapsto\sigma_1(r_2),
\qquad
\sigma_2(r_0)\mapsto\sigma_2(r_2),
\qquad
\sigma_0(r_2)\mapsto\sigma_0(r_0)\]
defines a distance-preserving map on a 3-point set,
which can be extended to an isometry of $M$.
By uniqueness of geodesics, we have that $\sigma_1(t)=\sigma_2(t)$ and $s(t)=0$ if $t\le r_2-r_0$ --- a contradiction.

The same argument shows that $s$ is essentially the same for all choices of bifurcating geodesics.
More precisely, if $s'$ is a function obtained the same way for another pair of bifurcating geodesics, then $s=s'$ on the common domain.
Indeed assume $s(r)=s'(r')$  and $\sigma'_0$, $\sigma'_1$, and $\sigma'_2$ are the corresponding parametrizations for $s'$.
Then $\sigma'_1(r')\mapsto\sigma_1(r)$, $\sigma'_2(r')\mapsto\sigma_2(r)$, and
$\sigma'_0(r)\mapsto\sigma_0(r')$
%\[\sigma'_1(r')\mapsto\sigma_1(r), \qquad \sigma'_2(r')\mapsto\sigma_2(r), \qquad \sigma'_0(r)\mapsto\sigma_0(r')\]
defines a distance-preserving map, which extends to an isometry of $M$, implying $r=r'$.
Since both functions are continuous and strictly increasing from $0$, it follows that \(s=s'\) on their common domain.



Fix a point $p\in M$ and $r>0$.
Given a point $x$, denote by $x_r$ the point on $[px]_M$ such that $|p-x_r|=r$; it is uniquely defined if $r<|p-x|_M<R$.
Observe that for any small $\eps>0$ there is $\delta>0$ such that
\[r+\eps<|p-x|_M<R,\quad r+\eps<|p-y|_M<R,\quad\text{and}\quad|x-y|<\delta\]
imply that $x_r=y_r$.
Indeed, let $a=|p-x|_M$; we can assume that $a\le |p-y|_M$.
Since $s$ is continuous and strictly increasing from $0$, we can choose $\delta>0$ such that $s(\eps)>2\cdot \delta$.
By the triangle inequality, $|x-y|<\delta$ $\Rightarrow$ $|x-y_a|<2\cdot\delta$,
and then $s(\eps)>2\cdot \delta$ $\Rightarrow$ $x_r=y_r$.

Choose a geodesic triangle $[pxy]_M$ in $M$ such that $|p-x|_M<\tfrac R2$, $|p-y|_M<\tfrac R2$;
let $r=\tfrac12\cdot(|p-x|_M+|p-y|_M-|x-y|_M)$.
Let us show that $[pxy]_M$ is a tripod.
If not, then $|p-z|_M>r$ for any $z\in [xy]_M$;
note also that $|p-z|<R$.
From above, $z\mapsto z_r$ locally constant and therefore constant on~$[xy]_M$.
It follows that $x_r=y_r\in [px]_M\cap[py]_M$.
Since $|x-x_r|_M+|y-y_r|_M=|x-y|_M$, we have $[xy]_M\subset [px]_M\cup[py]_M$ --- a contradiction.
Thus every sufficiently small triangle in $M$ is a tripod, and the statement follows. \qeds

Let us define \emph{local geodesic} as a locally distance-preserving map from an interval to a metric space.
A geodesic space $M$ will be called \emph{geodesically complete} if any nontrivial geodesic in $M$ can be extended to a local geodesic defined on the whole real line.

\begin{thm}{Lemma}
Any all-set-homogeneous geodesic space $M$ is geodesically complete.
\end{thm}

\parit{Proof.}
Choose a geodesic $\gamma$ in $M$; suppose it is defined on an interval $\II$.

Choose a subinterval $\JJ\subset \II$ and its shift $\JJ'\subset \II$ by $a>0$ such that $\II=\JJ\cup\JJ'$.
The map $\gamma(t)\mapsto \gamma(t+a)$ extends to an isometry $F $ of $M$,
and we can extend $\gamma$ by a local geodesic defined by $\bar\gamma(t+n\cdot a)\df F^n\circ \gamma(t)$
for $t\in \JJ$ and integer~$n$.
\qeds

\begin{thm}{Claim}
Any geodesically complete metric tree $T$ is complete.
\end{thm}

\parit{Proof.}
Assume there is a nonconverging Cauchy sequence $x_0,x_1,\ldots$ in~$T$.
Let $\ell=\lim |x_0-x_n|_T$; note that $\ell>0$.

Denote by $\gamma_n$ the geodesic from $x_0$ to $x_n$ that is parametrized from $x_0$.
If $0\le t<\ell$, then the sequence $\gamma_n(t)$ stabilises; that is, $\gamma_i(t)=\gamma_j(t)$ for all sufficiently large $i$ and $j$.
Defining $\gamma_\infty(t)$ as $\gamma_n(t)$ for sufficiently large $n$, produces a geodesic $\gamma_\infty \colon[0,\ell)\to T$.
Since $T$ is geodesically complete, $\gamma_\infty$ extends to $[0,\ell]$, but $x_0,x_1,\dots$ converge to $\gamma_\infty(\ell)$ --- a contradiction.
\qeds

\parit{Proof of the theorem.}
Let $M$ be an all-set-homogeneous geodesic space with locally unique geodesics.
If geodesics in $M$ do not bifurcate, then, by Birkhoff's theorem, $M$ is classical.
Otherwise, the proposition implies that $M$ is locally a tree.
Existence of bifurcation implies that the local tree has valence at least $3$ at some (and therefore any) point.

Let us show that $M$ is a metric tree; in other words, $M$ does not contain an embedded circle.

Assume the contrary; let $2\cdot R$ be the greatest lower bound of lengths of embedded circles in $M$.
Since $M$ is locally a tree, homogeneity implies that $R>0$.
Note that an $\tfrac R2$-neighborhood of any point in $M$ is a tree with valence at least $3$ (indeed, any nontripod triangle $[pxy]_M$ in this neighborhood would contain an embedded circle of length less than $2\cdot R$).
Furthermore, if $|x-y|_M\z<R$, then $[xy]_M$ is uniquely defined (again, distinct geodesics from $x$ to $y$ would produce an embedded circle of length less than $2\cdot R$).

It follows that any equilateral triangle in $M$ with side $s<R$ is a tripod.
Indeed, choose a point $p$ and three points $x$, $y$, and $z$ in different components of its punctured $\tfrac R2$-neighborhood at distance $\tfrac s2$ from $p$.
Note that $[xyz]_M$ is a tripod with side~$s$; by 3-point homogeneity the same holds for any triangle with side $s$.

Now choose an embedded circle $\Sigma$ of length slightly above $2\cdot R$.
Choose points $x,y,z$ that divide $\Sigma$ into three equal arcs of length $s$; we can assume  $s<R$.
Note that $|x-y|_M=s$; otherwise an arc of $\Sigma$ with $[xy]_M$ contains a circle of length less than $2\cdot R$, which is impossible.
It follows that $[xyz]_M$ is an equilateral triangle of side $s$, which is not a tripod --- a contradiction.

Now, $M$ is an all-set-homogeneous metric tree; in particular, it has the same valence at every point.
The lemma and the claim imply that $M$ is complete.%
\footnote{Pénélope Azuelos has shown that homogeneity is not sufficient \cite{azuelos2025}: \emph{there are noncomplete 2-point homogeneous metric trees  of any valence $n\ge 3$}.}
It follows that $M$ is a universal metric tree;
it remains to show that it has finite valence.

Assume the valence is infinite.
Then there is an infinite equilateral set $A\z\subset M$; indeed, one can take points at a fixed distance in distinct branches of the tree.
Extend $A$ to a maximal equilateral set  $\bar A$ with respect to inclusion.
Choose an isometry $f$ from $\bar A$ to its proper subset.
Note that $f$ cannot be extended to an isometry $F$ of $M$; otherwise $F^{-1}(a)$ for $a\in \bar A\setminus f(\bar A)$ can be added to $\bar A$, which is already maximal.
Therefore, $M$ is not all-set-homogeneous --- a contradiction.
\qeds
